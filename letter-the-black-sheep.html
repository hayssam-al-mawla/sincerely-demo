A letter from Katherine, November 10, 2026.

A 6 minute read

The black sheep.

November 10, 2026

A few days ago, I was talking with a friend, someone I care about and deeply admire. We were talking about family, about the things we carry through our lives, and about her journey of breaking a cycle of intergenerational trauma.

At one point, she referred to herself as the black sheep of the family.

Those words stopped me. I have never seen her as a black sheep. I see an extraordinary mother, an incredibly curious, creative and talented human being, and someone who is beautiful inside and out. Someone who has found the courage to journey deeply within herself, to embrace more of who she truly is, and to live from that place. Her essence. Her uniqueness. Her beauty.

I asked her, “Does claiming your authentic self mean that you’re the black sheep of the family?”

Her answer was “yes”.

I have been contemplating that conversation ever since.

I had never thought of myself as the black sheep of my family either. And yet, when I began the journey to understand and eventually affirm my gender, I was confronting something I had known from a very young age. I was different. I knew what I was doing as an adult was rare and could be deeply disruptive to people I loved. I carried fears of judgement, rejection and abandonment. I deeply contemplated suicide. I had no idea how to take on the journey or how my family, friends, colleagues or community would respond.

Perhaps some of them did see me as the black sheep. I really don’t know.

What I do know is that I wasn’t trying to be different. I was trying to understand something deep within me that had been there since childhood. Something I had never fully understood or known how to express. And that journey became so much more than affirming my gender. It became a journey towards understanding, accepting and loving more of who I am. My essence.

As I’ve reflected on my friend’s words, I’ve become curious about the expression itself.

Historically, naturally white wool was more commercially desirable because it could be dyed into so many colours. Think of all the beauty created through fashion, textiles, design and art. And yet, there is something interesting about how we assign value. White wool could be transformed, transmuted, even muted into what someone else wanted it to be. A performance shaped by another’s expectations.

Black wool, with its own natural colour, was considered less desirable for some of those purposes. And it too can be woven into beautiful textiles, designs and creations while retaining its natural colour. Its natural essence. The more I thought about it, the more I realised that both black and white wool are beautiful expressions of nature, of life, each with its own qualities and perfection. It was we humans who judged one as more valuable based on what we wanted it to become.

And that got me reflecting again about ourselves.

How much of our lives do we spend learning to become what others want or expect us to be? From childhood, we learn the ways of our families, our communities, our workplaces and the broader human family. We learn what is acceptable, what earns approval, what helps us belong. Sometimes we become so good at fitting in that we lose sight of parts of ourselves that were always there. Our uniqueness and beauty.

Perhaps the so-called black sheep is someone who has begun to release those expectations and live more fully from their nature, their natural essence. Their uniqueness becomes visible, and sometimes that makes others uncomfortable. Witnessing someone living more authentically can stir something deep within us, something we don’t yet understand or haven’t found the courage to express ourselves. We may react defensively and judgementally to what feels unfamiliar, or to something within ourselves that is quietly asking to be explored, while fear encourages us to resist. The person standing before us becomes a mirror, reflecting parts of our own essence that are longing to be seen.

And there is a beautiful gift in that.

My friend spoke about healing intergenerational trauma, about breaking a cycle. I’ve been thinking about the word healing too. It can suggest that something was broken and needed to be fixed.

What if our original essence was never broken? What if it was perfect, whole and complete?

What if, through our experiences of judgement, rejection, shame and trying to fit in, we accumulated layers of protection around something that was always beautiful and whole? It’s like scar tissue, formed through experiences that hurt us and through the ways we learned to protect ourselves in a family, group or world that values fitting in to what it values.

Perhaps healing is less about fixing ourselves and more about gently releasing what no longer serves us. Learning to understand why those layers formed, finding compassion for ourselves and the people who came before us, and allowing more of our original essence to become visible once again.

I think about my own parents and ancestors, whose lives were shaped by war, displacement, loss and survival. I have come to understand how fear, silence and ways of protecting ourselves can travel through generations. And how love, courage and resilience travel too.

What if breaking a cycle is an expression of love for our family and our ancestors? A releasing of something we no longer need to carry, while honouring the lives and experiences that came before us.

And what if, in doing so, we create more room for the generations that follow to simply be themselves?

This brings me back to something I explored in my earlier letter, Entelechy: A Doorway. Aristotle spoke of Entelechy, an ancient Greek word that has come to mean something deeply personal to me. The quiet longing within us to know and express what has always been there.

Joseph Campbell said, “The privilege of a lifetime is being who you are.”

I love that word, being. Being.

Perhaps the privilege of this lifetime is to discover and love the beautiful being who has been there all along, rather than spend our lives trying to become someone more acceptable to others.

And as we learn to love more of ourselves, perhaps something else begins to happen.

I think about the love I have received from my children, my friends and so many others throughout my journey. Their love helped me find more love within myself. And as I have learned to love myself more deeply, I have found a greater capacity to offer that love to others.

When someone finds the courage to release what has obscured their natural essence, their light becomes more visible. And perhaps that light becomes a mirror for someone else, awakening a longing to live more freely, more truthfully, more lovingly.

They may begin their own journey, in their own way, towards their own beautiful essence.

I find this knowing extraordinarily beautiful. Each of us carries our own unique gifts, our visible and invisible dimensions of human uniqueness, our own light. As we find the courage to live from that deeper place, perhaps we contribute to something much larger than our individual lives. A collective journey towards greater compassion, greater wholeness and greater love.

I keep coming back to my friend and the conversation that started all of this.

She saw herself as the black sheep of her family. I saw her beauty, her courage, her truth. I saw someone who had journeyed deeply within herself, who was breaking a cycle and allowing more of her natural essence to shine into this world and beyond.

The black sheep was never less beautiful or less whole than the rest of the flock. We simply hadn’t learned to see its profound beauty. Its light.

The black sheep is sometimes someone who has found the courage to stop hiding their natural colour, to reclaim the love and wholeness that have always been within them, and to let their light shine.

What a beautiful gift to give to ourselves.

Thank you, Black Sheep.

Sincerely, Katherine.
