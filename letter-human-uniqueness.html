Sincerely Letters

A letter from Katherine, October 27, 2026.

Human Uniqueness.

October 27, 2026

This past Monday, I had the honour and privilege of joining the Human Resources Professionals Association (HRPA) for its annual Summit, The Human Advantage, Canada’s largest gathering of human resources professionals. I was invited by Desjardins to join Lance Lambert for a conversation about unlocking human potential, and what it means to live and lead in a world that is experiencing immense stress and changing so profoundly.

What a beautiful opportunity to share some of the lessons and reflections from my journey through life and leadership and the work of Sincerely, and to explore together what might become possible for humanity and our world.

Afterwards, I spent time meeting people and signing copies of my book, Sincerely, Katherine. I was deeply moved by the warmth, kindness and openness of those who came forward. Some entrusted me with deeply personal parts of their lives. Those exchanges are sacred to me, and I carry them with tremendous gratitude.

I’ve been thinking about those conversations all week. What does it mean to be human? And what makes each and every one of us so beautifully unique, especially now, in a world increasingly shaped by AI, stress and conflict?

For much of my life, I’ve struggled with the word diversity. Not with the importance of honouring our differences, but with the word itself. To me, it has often suggested division, or even the act of dividing.

I’ve also struggled with the labels we place on ourselves and one another. They can help us describe aspects of who we are, yet no label, identity, role or characteristic can possibly contain a whole human being. It’s something I have been making sense of in my own life.

I’m a woman who affirmed her gender. I’m also a mother, a daughter, a sister, a friend, a writer, a former bank CEO, a deeply curious, playful and loving soul, and so much more. None of these descriptions, individually or together, fully captures the whole human being I am. The same is true of every one of us.

Somewhere along my journey, an expression came to me that felt more loving, more expansive and more reflective of how I’ve come to understand humanity.

Visible and invisible dimensions of human uniqueness.

I love the word human. It begins with what we share: our desire to feel safe, to love and be loved, to experience belonging, to find meaning and to contribute to something beyond ourselves. Our shared humanity does not require sameness.

I love the word uniqueness. It speaks to something special, something beautiful, something worthy of being discovered, understood, loved and allowed to belong.

The visible and invisible dimensions speak to the extraordinary fullness and depth of every human being. So much of who we are cannot be seen from the outside: our histories, fears and dreams; the ways we think and perceive the world; our talents and gifts; our hopes and aspirations; the stories and experiences that have shaped us; and the parts of ourselves we may only reveal when we feel safe enough to do so.

Human uniqueness, for me, is not another way of categorising people. It’s the opposite. It is a reminder that no category can ever contain a human being.

I’ve come to see the whole human through what I call the Three-Self Architecture: our physical and inherited self, our lived-experience self and our essential self. Our physical and inherited self carries the characteristics and attributes we arrive in this world with. Our lived-experience self is shaped through our relationships, culture, education, work, joys, hardships, achievements and the countless experiences that shape our lives.

And then there is our essential self, that beautiful spark within us from birth, with our curiosity, imagination, creativity, playfulness, sensitivity, intuition and capacity to love. I often think about the little child within each of us, fiercely curious, exploring, playing, creating and loving.

Then life met us.

Our families, schools, religions, communities and workplaces began teaching us how to behave, how to succeed and how to belong. Some of those lessons were beautiful gifts. Others taught us that certain parts of ourselves were not welcome, so we learned to protect ourselves and to perform to fit in.

Some of the parts we tucked away began to carry guilt or shame because expressing them had once brought judgement, rejection or a loss of safety and belonging. Hiding and adapting may have been exactly what we needed to do at that moment in our lives. Yet what once protected us can eventually separate us from our deepest and truest self, our own essence.

Some parts of ourselves may remain protected for very good reasons. Feeling safe should never come with an expectation that we disclose, confront or express something before we are ready.

Whether in a family, a friendship or a workplace, we can create conditions of love, care and belonging without asking another person to reveal what they wish to hold privately. Their story remains theirs.

When I affirmed my gender publicly in 2019, a video was shared with approximately 80,000 colleagues. I was terrified. I expected judgement and rejection. I worried about my career. More than anything, I worried about hurting the people I loved.

The year before, that fear had taken me to one of the darkest periods of my life, to wanting life to be over. What happened next changed me profoundly.

I experienced an extraordinary outpouring of love, kindness and compassion, and then people began trusting me with deeply personal parts of their own lives. I had spent decades working with people and considered myself a compassionate and empathetic leader, yet I had no idea how much hurt, fear, longing, beauty and human experience was sitting quietly all around me.

I was humbled to my core. I became less judgemental, more curious and more compassionate. I listened differently, and I began to understand something I had missed through much of my career: there was so much more in people than I had learned to see.

I also began to understand how many of us carry fear, guilt or shame around the parts of ourselves we have learned to hide.

I’ve come to think of our human journey as one towards greater coherence. Not becoming some better or more acceptable version of ourselves, but learning to see, reconcile and love more of the whole human being who is already here.

Grieving what needs to be grieved, healing what asks to be healed, gently releasing what no longer serves us, and allowing more of our natural essence and whole self to find expression through our lives.

Unlocking human potential begins there, not by putting more into people, but by creating conditions where people have greater freedom to be themselves and bring forward their gifts, in their own way and in their own time.

I find this question particularly important now. We are living through a consequential moment in human history. Social fracture, economic inequality and the degradation of our planet are profound challenges, all unfolding within an overwhelming environment of information and disinformation.

Artificial intelligence is accelerating all of it. I don’t see AI as inherently good or bad. I see it as an extraordinary accelerator of the orientation from which we choose to use it.

Used from fear, scarcity, competition and extraction, it can accelerate those things, making us even more efficient at extracting value from people, communities and our planet. Used from a different orientation, it can help create greater space for human creativity, imagination, relationship, discernment and contribution, and help us solve problems that have challenged humanity for generations.

The technology will continue to become more capable, which leaves us with a deeply human question: what are we behaving from, and what are we putting all of this extraordinary capability in service of?

For generations, we’ve built many of our organisations around efficiency, productivity, standardisation and control. We’ve often asked human beings to perform and adapt themselves to the systems we created.

This moment gives us an opportunity to turn that question around. How might we build and steward organisations where the whole human can flourish?

I’ve had the privilege of exploring that question with extraordinary people throughout my career. At RBC, 25,000 of us were navigating a profound digital transformation together, and people were understandably worried about what technology might mean for their jobs and their families.

Our shared aspiration became larger than digitising transactions. We wanted to help move work away from the purely transactional and create greater opportunity for people to bring more of their uniquely human gifts into the lives of the Canadians we served: empathy, curiosity, context, judgement, understanding and meaningful advice.

We certainly didn’t get everything right, and I’ve made more than my share of mistakes throughout my career. Yet that experience reinforced something I continue to believe deeply. There are extraordinary gifts inside people that become even more important as technology becomes more capable.

This changes what it means to lead. Leadership carries a responsibility to see those gifts, nurture them and bring them together in service of something meaningful.

Management is an accountability we can be given, while leadership is something recognised through the experiences others have of us. A manager manages. A leader may be recognised for their wisdom, for the vision and inspiration they offer, for the morality they embody when choices are difficult, for their courage and discernment, and for their ability to recognise beautiful gifts within others, sometimes before those people can see the gifts within themselves.

Most importantly, leadership is about bringing those gifts together in a movement towards something greater than ourselves.

Leadership, for me, is increasingly about stewardship. It carries responsibility for something greater, for nurturing life itself and creating the conditions in which the whole human can flourish, while holding responsibility for the wider community and living world of which we are part.

Another responsibility of leadership is to create the field, the conditions of safety, trust and belonging, while remembering that each person retains sovereignty over what they share, what they hold privately and when.

Belonging should never require disclosure. Disclosure is sacred.

This is part of the world I hope we can continue to build: one that is more socially and economically inclusive, where we learn to live more harmoniously with ourselves, each other and our planet, and where the systems we build support that harmony rather than exploit and fracture it.

None of this removes the difficult responsibilities of leadership. I learned that painfully while working alongside thousands of colleagues across nineteen Caribbean countries to preserve the future of a bank and save as many jobs as possible.

Together, we accomplished something important, and the restructuring required decisions that affected people, families and communities in ways that broke my heart. Compassion does not relieve us of the responsibility to make difficult decisions. It asks more of us.

It asks us to create space to feel, reflect, listen, observe and understand their consequences; to honour the dignity of the people, communities and living systems affected; and to remain human when the pressures around us generate fear and pressure within us, making it tempting not to.

Financial performance matters. Accountability matters. Innovation matters. None of these should become ends unto themselves.

What are they in service of?

Leadership for today and tomorrow asks us to hold a vision for something better for all, while remaining deeply present to the people, communities and living world around us. It asks us to listen, remain curious, see the whole human being rather than the label, and create the safety and belonging that allow more of another person’s uniqueness to find expression, if and when they choose.

Then it asks us to bring all of those gifts together towards something none of us could create alone.

I think back to the people I met after the HRPA conversation. Each a whole human being, carrying a lifetime of experiences, a depth I could never fully know, and gifts that are uniquely their own.

What a privilege it is when another human being allows us to see a little more of who they are.

Unlocking human potential begins there: learning to see and love more of the whole human being, within ourselves and within one another, and bringing our visible and invisible dimensions of human uniqueness together in service of something greater than ourselves.

A world where the whole human can flourish. A world where humanity and all life can flourish together.

Sincerely, Katherine.
